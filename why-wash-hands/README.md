## 🎯 The Question

Grown-ups tell you to wash your hands all day long. Before you eat. After you
play. After you touch almost anything.

**A six-year-old decided to find out whether any of that was true.**

Not by reading about it. By playing for an entire day without washing her
hands, rinsing them into a bowl that evening, and putting that water under a
microscope.

## 🔬 What She Found

The water looked clean in the bowl. It was not.

**Bacteria. Paramecium. A crowd of them**, swimming across a screen in a first
grade living room, every one of them off a single day of one pair of hands.

| | |
|---|---|
| 🦠 **The sample** | One day of unwashed hands, rinsed into a bowl |
| 🔎 **The method** | A real optical microscope, piped to a computer screen |
| 🧫 **The find** | Bacteria and paramecium, alive and moving |
| 🧼 **The answer** | Water alone does nothing. Soap or alcohol stops them |

## 🧠 Wrong Twice, Then Right

This is the part that won it.

She did not guess correctly and stop. She guessed **three times**, and the page
keeps all three in the order she made them, because the wrong ones are the
project.

| | Her guess | What the microscope said |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | We don't need to wash our hands… that much! | ❌ Wrong. Her hands were covered |
| **2** | Maybe I should wash with water | ❌ Wrong again. Water just moves germs around |
| **3** | Maybe I should wash with soap or alcohol | ✅ **Right.** The alcohol killed them instantly |

> *"I was wrong twice before I got there, and that is the part I liked best.
> Being wrong was not the end of my project. It was how my project kept
> going."*

A first grader ran a hypothesis, broke it on evidence, formed a new one, broke
that one too, and kept going. **That is the scientific method, done properly,
at age six.**
