Experiment 1: does a cold leaf hold its water at Martian pressure?

Version 5 rests on one calculation. Above freezing, the water vapour inside a leaf is at a higher pressure than the whole Martian atmosphere, so the leaf does not wilt, it boils. If that is right, a leaf at Martian pressure should behave completely differently either side of zero degrees. This experiment looks for that line, and it needs one afternoon.

What we would be asking of you
The question Held at 6 millibars, does a leaf lose water slowly below freezing and violently above it, and does the change happen where the physics says it should?
Bench space A vacuum chamber, and a shelf for the plants
How long One afternoon per run, and a few runs
Biosafety BSL-1. Plants and a pump. Nothing regulated, nothing hazardous
From the lab A vacuum chamber that reaches a few millibars, a cold plate or a way to hold samples at set temperatures, and a scale reading to 0.001 g inside or immediately outside the chamber
From us The plants, the protocol, and the analysis
Student on site One session per run

Why this one first

What we are looking at What it says
What is known Water boils at about 0 °C at 6 millibars. That is in every physics textbook
Why the gap matters A leaf is not an open dish of water. Its water is held in cell walls and behind a waxy skin, and both of those change how easily water leaves. Nobody has measured where the line actually falls for a leaf
What this decides Whether version 5’s central claim is true, and therefore whether a cold, slow plant is the only possible answer or just one of several

Design

Five temperatures, three leaves each, 15 samples per run, plus controls at ordinary pressure.

Held at What the physics predicts
-20 °C slow, steady water loss
-10 °C slow, steady water loss
-2 °C slow, still below the line
+5 °C above the line. Rapid, visible loss
+20 °C far above the line. Very rapid loss
  • The point of the ladder is to find a step, not a slope. If water simply leaves faster when it is warmer, that is ordinary evaporation and our claim adds nothing. If there is a sharp change between -2 °C and +5 °C, that is boiling. That is the result we are looking for.
  • Controls at ordinary pressure, at the same temperatures, so that whatever the cold alone does to a leaf can be separated from what the pressure does.
  • Three leaf types, because the waxy skin is the thing most likely to move the line: an ordinary crop leaf, a desert succulent, and a moss.

On the pressure. Six millibars is a soft vacuum. Any chamber used for freeze drying or degassing reaches it easily, and nothing here needs a high vacuum rig.

Setup

Why
1 Cut leaves of a consistent size and weigh each one
2 Bring each sample to its assigned temperature before pumping down so the temperature is settled and the change is not a thawing artefact
3 Pump to 6 millibars as quickly as the chamber allows, and hold
4 Weigh at fixed intervals, and film at least one run at each temperature
5 Repeat the whole ladder at ordinary pressure as the control

What happens, and when

When What to do
Minute 0 Starting weight, starting temperature, pump down
Every few minutes Weight, and a note on appearance. Bubbling, collapse or frothing all get written down
End of run Final weight. Then dry every sample fully so the water lost can be given as a fraction of the water there was
After Rewet the samples and see which recover, which is a free extra result

What we expect to see

Where to look What we expect
Below freezing Slow loss, similar at all three cold temperatures
Above freezing A large jump. Loss many times faster, and visible bubbling or collapse
The number that matters Where the jump sits. If it sits near 0 °C, the physics holds for real leaves
What would surprise us The jump sitting well above 0 °C. That would mean cell walls hold water more tightly than free water, and a Martian plant could be warmer than we think, which would be very good news
What would end it No jump at all, just a smooth slope. Then boiling is not a special threat, our central claim is wrong, and version 5’s whole argument for a cold plant collapses

Either outcome is worth having. This is the rare experiment where being wrong would make the design easier rather than harder.

Recording rules

  • Label by number and keep the key aside until every sample is weighed.
  • Write down what is seen, not what it means. A sample that slips, thaws or is knocked gets a note on the day, and the note stays in the report.
  • Keep the film. If a leaf boils, the film is the clearest result this project will ever produce.

The sheets, blank and filled in

Spreadsheets, printed and filled in by hand at the bench, then typed up exactly as written. Every column is defined in the codebook. They are empty, because nobody has run this yet.

The sheet What goes in it
run_log.csv one row per run. Leaf type, the pressure asked for and the pressure reached, and how long the pump took
samples.csv which leaf was held at which temperature and pressure, its area and its starting weight
mass_log.csv the weight every few minutes, and what the leaf looked like. The sheet the answer comes from
dry_mass.csv the final dry weight, so the loss can be given as a share of the water there was
recovery.csv whether the leaf came back after being rewetted

The same sheets also exist with invented numbers in them, made by a script, so the sheets can be checked before there is real data. This set is the expected outcome: a sharp step between minus two and plus five at six millibars, and no step at ordinary pressure. run_log · samples · mass_log · dry_mass · recovery · a dry run, not a result
