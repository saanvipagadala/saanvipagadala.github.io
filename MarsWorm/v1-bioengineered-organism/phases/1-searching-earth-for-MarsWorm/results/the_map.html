How to read this map

The whole job of a Mars soil-maker is split into 23 jobs: 13 for staying alive on Mars, 10 for turning dust into soil. Every dot is a real organism, placed by how many of those jobs it can fill, across for survival, up for soil enrichment.

One point per job, and it stops there. Having forty starch enzymes is not better than having one: the job is either covered or it is not. That cap is deliberate: counting genes instead would just rank organisms by how much has been written about them.

The gold star at the top right is the goal: every job filled. Nothing on Earth is there.

The worm is a design, not an organism

It fills every job by construction: it was built by filling them, so a lower score would just mean the work was unfinished. The claim worth making is narrower: no single organism reaches that corner, and a combination of real genes can. Whether it works inside a living cell is what the later stages are for.

The donors are not the impressive ones

The dots still showing are the organisms our genes actually came from, and most of them are nowhere near the corner. A rubber tree and a barley plant score 1 out of 23 each; they are here because each gave one gene that no better-scoring organism could. Being the best organism overall and having the best protein for one job are different things, which is exactly why the gene search never filtered by organism.

Why one point per job

The score is capped on purpose. Counting genes instead would rank organisms by how much has been written about them, which is how Deinococcus radiodurans, the reference organism for radiation resistance, once failed to rank for radiation resistance.

A zero can mean no data

An organism nobody has sequenced looks empty here. But that does not weaken what the map shows, because the bias runs the wrong way to help: the organisms scoring highest are the best-studied on Earth. If they cannot do both, the gap is real.
