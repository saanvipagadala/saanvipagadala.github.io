Step 4. Destroy the perchlorate, remove the salt

Azospira, a bacterium that lives on perchlorate, takes it apart in a dark tank. What is left is plain salt, and the salt is taken out of the water and poured onto Mars.

How Azospira takes the perchlorate apart

  • What perchlorate is. One chlorine atom with four oxygen atoms clamped onto it. To make it harmless, all four oxygens have to be pulled off.
  • Pulling oxygen off something takes hydrogen. That is why hydrogen is bubbled into this tank. Put hydrogen and perchlorate together in water on their own, though, and nothing happens for a thousand years.
  • Azospira makes it happen. Its enzymes bring the hydrogen and the perchlorate together, and the bacterium lives on the energy that is released. Hydrogen is the fuel. Azospira is the engine.
  • The sour acids from step 3 are what Azospira builds its body from. Hydrogen is spent on the stripping. The acids are carbon, and Azospira cannot make carbon out of the air the way Rhodobacter in step 3 does, so it has to be given some.
  • Azospira breathes the oxygen it pulls off. The four oxygens come off as ordinary breathable oxygen, inside the cell, and that is what it breathes while it works.
  • What is left is chloride. Chloride is the harmless half of table salt, and it stays dissolved in the water.

Why the salt has to come out of the water

  • Because we washed the regolith first, the chloride is in the water, not in the soil. In a design where the microbe works inside the soil, the salt would have nowhere to go but the bed you are trying to farm.
  • But the water cannot go round for ever with the salt in it. After one pass through the tank it is already saltier than a radish will put up with.
  • So membranes pull the chloride out. A little electricity drives them, and nothing is boiled. Clean water comes out one side. A small amount of very salty water, brine, comes out the other.

Why the brine is poured onto Mars

  • It adds nothing that is not already there. Mars is covered in chloride and perchlorate across the whole planet, in far larger amounts than we could ever pour out. We are handing back a little of what we took, in a gentler form than we took it.
  • The water cost is small and mostly paid back. Only a few litres leave with the brine per tonne of regolith. And the tank makes fresh water as it works, because pulling oxygen off perchlorate with hydrogen makes water.
  • Keeping the brine would be worse. A store of brine inside a sealed habitat is a leak waiting to happen, and it grows with every batch.
  • Two rules go with it. Sterilise the brine before it goes out, because the rule for Mars is about not seeding it with Earth life, not about salt. And pick one marked spot, away from anywhere anyone will dig.

Why it matters

  • The perchlorate is now destroyed, not stored. Step 1 only moved it out of the regolith and into the water. This step takes it apart, and it is gone for good.
