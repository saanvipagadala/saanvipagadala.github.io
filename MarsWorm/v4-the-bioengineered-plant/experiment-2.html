Experiment 2: does cleaning the pot rescue the plant?

Experiment 1 asks whether the chlorate does its damage the way we say. This one asks whether removing it fixes the plant. It does that without engineering anything, by putting a live bacterium in the pot to do the job the gene would do.

What we would be asking of you
The question If a perchlorate-eating bacterium destroys the chlorate in the pot, does a radish grow normally in Martian simulant?
Bench space 16 pots on a plant bench
How long Six weeks, because the bacterium is given two weeks before sowing
Biosafety BSL-1. One ordinary soil bacterium, plus a few grams of chlorate
From the lab A plant bench, a drying oven, a scale, and a way to measure chlorate and chloride
From us Everything else
Student on site A short session a week

Why this matters

What we are looking at What it says
What the design says A gene in the root splits chlorite into chloride and oxygen, so the plant never loses its nitrogen enzyme
What this does instead Puts Azospira suillum, which carries that enzyme naturally, into the pot. The chemistry is the same. Only the address is different
Why that is worth doing It tests the value of job 1 with no genetic modification, no licence and no waiting. If cleaning the pot does not rescue the plant, putting the gene in the plant will not either

Design

Four treatments, four pots each, 16 pots.

Pot Simulant Chlorate Bacterium
A yes yes yes
B yes yes no
C yes no yes
D yes no no
  • Pot A is the design. Pot B is Martian dirt left alone.
  • Pot C asks whether the bacterium itself helps or harms, separately from the chlorate. A bacterium in the root zone is not a neutral thing. If pot C beats pot D, then some of pot A’s result belongs to the bacterium being there, not to the cleaning.
  • Pot D is the clean yardstick.

Materials

What How much
We bring
Azospira suillum PS, from a culture collection one vial, revived in advance
MGS-1 Mars regolith simulant 8 kg
Radish seed, pots, trays, labels 16 pots
A nitrogen-free nutrient solution and nitrate salt made up in advance
Chlorate and chloride test strips, as a running check one pack each
Ordered to your address
Sodium chlorate 10 g, laboratory grade
The lab already has
A plant bench with lights on a timer about 1 m
Drying oven, scale to 0.001 g
An ion chromatograph or an ion selective electrode for the chlorate and chloride numbers

Setup

Why
1 Weigh the same simulant into every pot, and stir the dissolved chlorate through A and B
2 Add the bacterium to A and C. B and D get the same volume of sterile liquid so the only difference is whether it is alive
3 Keep every pot damp and dark for two weeks, with no plant the bacterium gets time to work before the radish arrives
4 Sample every pot for chlorate and chloride, then sow this is the number that says whether the cleaning happened
5 Randomise on the bench and keep the key aside

What happens, and when

When What to do
Day 0 to 14 Damp and dark, no plants. Weekly chlorate and chloride from every pot
Day 14 Sow. Record the chlorate and chloride each pot starts its plant life with
Weekly after that Count plants, measure leaf area, note colour. Water to the same weight
Day 42 Harvest. Dry and weigh roots and leaves separately. Final soil chlorate and chloride

What we expect to see

Where to look What we expect
By day 14 Chlorate falling in pot A and not in pot B, with chloride rising to match
Pot A Radishes close to pot D. The chlorate is gone before the seed goes in
Pot B Small pale plants, as in experiment 1
Pot C Close to pot D, or a little better if the bacterium helps the root
What would surprise us Chlorate falling in pot A but the plants staying sick. That would mean something else in Martian simulant is doing the damage, and the poison is not the whole story
What would end it No chlorate removed in pot A. Version 3’s experiment 1 asks the same question of the same bacterium, so a failure here is a failure for two versions at once

Recording rules

  • Label by number and keep the key aside until every measurement is done.
  • Write down what is seen, not what it means. Anything that goes wrong gets a note on the day, and the note stays in the report.

The sheets, blank and filled in

Spreadsheets, printed and filled in by hand at the bench, then typed up exactly as written. Every column is defined in the codebook. They are empty, because nobody has run this yet.

The sheet What goes in it
pots.csv which pot is which treatment, and where it sits on the bench
soil.csv chlorate and chloride in the dirt, week by week. This is what says the cleaning happened
growth.csv the weekly count, leaf area and colour, once the radishes are in
harvest.csv dry weights of root and leaf at day 42
care_log.csv the weekly watering and damp check

The same sheets also exist with invented numbers in them, made by a script, so the sheets can be checked before there is real data. This set is the expected outcome: the bacterium clears the chlorate in the first fortnight, and the radishes sown after it grow. pots · soil · growth · harvest · care_log · a dry run, not a result

The analysis reads those sheets and writes a report: charts, a verdict for each question, and its own checks on the data. The rules that decide the verdicts were fixed before any data existed. See what that report looks like, built from the invented sheets, and it says dry run in its own title
