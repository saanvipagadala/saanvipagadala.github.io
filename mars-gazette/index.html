Dear ORISE Judges,

MY NAME IS SAANVI, a 4th grader from California. During the Christmas break, I watched a YouTube documentary called "The Thinking Game" about Demis Hassabis, who founded Google DeepMind and won the Nobel Prize for solving one of science's hardest problems. It told his story from childhood to Nobel Prize, and I thought: I want to spend my life solving hard problems that matter.

What struck me most was how the documentary brought Demis's story to life. It did not just list his achievements. It took me back in time to his childhood, brought me to the present day showing his breakthrough, and then opened a window into the future of what AI might mean for humanity. It was engaging, emotional, and unforgettable. That is when it hit me: this is how I want to tell my story. This competition is asking me to imagine where I will be in 30 years. A documentary about my life would do exactly that: take the viewer back to when I was a little girl in California, bring them to my greatest achievement, and show what it means for the future of humanity. If I had time, I would have made a fully fictional video documentary. The next best thing is a front-page newspaper article from the year 2055, written as if it had already happened.

I chose Martian Colonization Bioengineering as my future career. It is the science of engineering living things (plants, animals, and the human body itself) to survive and thrive on Mars, and designing the habitats that make it all possible. It is one of the hardest and most important problems humanity will ever face: how do we grow food, protect human health, and build a living ecosystem on another planet? That is the kind of problem I want to spend my life solving.

I researched real NASA science, imagined Dr. Saanvi's daily life in vivid detail, cited reliable sources, and wrote the kind of article I hope someone will write about me one day. The result is the newspaper waiting for you below.

Please scroll down to read The Mars Gazette, July 4, 2055, a front-page newspaper article set 30 years in the future, imagining the day Dr. Saanvi makes history. Could this be real? Thank you for reading something a little different.

🌱 The Story of a Life Well Lived
Dr. Saanvi Makes Mars Bloom
How a Girl from California Who Once Cried Over a Tomato Seed Became the Scientist Who Brought Life to Another Planet
By Staff Reporter T. Aldrin  ·  Mars Base Alpha Correspondent  ·  July 4, 2055  ·  Special Anniversary Edition
Meet the Girl Who Changed Everything
Dr. Saanvi · Chief Bioengineer · Mars Base Alpha · 2055
Inspiration

THIRTY YEARS AGO, a seven-year-old girl in California planted a tomato seed in a paper cup on her bedroom windowsill. She checked it every morning before school and every night before bed. When the first tiny green shoot curled out of the soil, she burst into tears. Her mother asked, "Why are you crying? It is just a tomato." And little Saanvi said, "Because I made something alive." Nobody in that room could have imagined that one day, she would make something alive on Mars.

That girl is now Dr. Saanvi, Chief Martian Colonization Bioengineer at Mars Base Alpha, and today the Red Planet looks nothing like it did when she was born. There are gardens here. There are bees. There is the smell of rain. And it is all because of her.

Years before she ever set foot on Mars, young Saanvi watched a documentary about space and saw the barren, rust-colored desert stretching endlessly across the screen. No trees. No bees. No rain. No tomatoes. While everyone else felt sadness, she felt something different. She felt a challenge. "Somebody has to be the person who brings the first seed to Mars," she told herself. "I decided, right then, that person was going to be me."

Her role model was Sally Ride, the first American woman in space. "She once said, 'You can not be what you can not see.' When I saw Sally Ride in space, I saw myself on Mars. That is when I knew anything was possible."

"Somebody has to bring the first seed to Mars. I decided that person was going to be me."

Education

Dr. Saanvi earned her Bachelor of Science in Bioengineering from MIT, where she studied biology, genetics, chemistry, and environmental science. She then completed the Mars Life Sciences Fellowship at the Interplanetary Research Academy (IRA), a two-year program on designing living systems for space. "MIT taught me how life works. IRA taught me how to take life somewhere it has never been before."

Career

Today, Dr. Saanvi holds the title of Chief Martian Colonization Bioengineer at the Interplanetary BioSciences Institute (IBI), located at Mars Base Alpha, the largest human settlement on the Red Planet, home to 3,200 permanent residents. She leads the Life Systems Team of 40 scientists and engineers, responsible for making sure that plants, animals, and humans can all survive and thrive on Mars.

Daily Work: Life Sciences

No two days are the same for Dr. Saanvi, but her work covers three key areas every day:

🌱 Plants: Back in the 2020s, NASA scientists took the first small steps by growing lettuce, tomatoes, radishes, and chile peppers on the International Space Station.[1] Dr. Saanvi took that work 100 million miles further. She spent years engineering entirely new crop varieties built specifically for Martian soil, Martian gravity, and Martian light levels. Today she manages 14 species of Mars Crops, including potatoes, soybeans, and leafy greens. Every morning she walks the greenhouse rows, reads growth data on her tablet, and decides what gets planted next.

🐛 Animals: NASA proved decades ago that living creatures can survive in space.[2] Dr. Saanvi went much further. Today, honeybees and monarch butterflies drift between flowering crops inside the domes, pollinating just as they would in any meadow back on Earth. Beneath the soil, her proudest invention quietly does its work: the MarsWorm, a genetically engineered earthworm that converts Martian dirt into rich, living compost. "Healthy soil is the foundation of all life," she says. "Without it, none of this works." And at dusk, dwarf rabbits bred over ten generations for Mars's lower gravity[5] hop freely through the habitat corridors, each one named, each one loved. They are not dogs. But on a planet 140 million miles from Earth, they are close enough.

🧬 Humans: NASA knew for decades that low gravity causes astronauts to lose 1 to 1.5% of their bone density every single month.[4] Early astronauts had countermeasures, but they were designed for six-month missions, not lifetimes. Dr. Saanvi redesigned everything from scratch for people who will never return to Earth. Her daily exercise protocols, calcium-rich Mars diets, and bone-monitoring programs have kept every settler at Mars Base Alpha healthy for the past decade.

"Every morning I check on my plants, my animals, and my people. That is a good day's work."

🏠 Building Homes on Mars
Daily Work: Habitat Design

In the early 2020s, NASA began testing a bold idea on Earth: a 3D-printed Mars habitat called CHAPEA, where volunteers lived inside for a full year to practice what life on Mars would feel like.[6] It was a single small module. Dr. Saanvi took that single module and turned it into a city. Today, Mars Base Alpha spans seven interconnected bio-domes, each one designed, tested, and improved by her team over 15 years.

The challenge NASA identified decades ago was deadly radiation: Mars has no magnetic field, so its surface is continuously bombarded by harmful solar rays.[3] Dr. Saanvi solved this by developing a multi-layer shielding system that uses compressed Martian rock packed between polymer walls. Inside, the air is warm, oxygen-rich, and scented with growing things. Air processors convert carbon dioxide back into oxygen using the plants themselves. "The dome is alive," she says. "Every part supports every other part, just like a rainforest on Earth." Settlers walk around without spacesuits, something that once seemed impossible.

🎤 In Her Own Words
Inspiration
Q: Was there one moment that changed everything for you?
"Yes! When I was in 4th grade in California, I entered the ORISE Future in STEM Challenge. I wrote about becoming a Martian Colonization Bioengineer. I was so nervous. But I won first place! I was invited to present at NASA and the White House. Standing in those rooms, I thought: this is real. I am going to do this. I have been chasing that feeling every single day since."
Q: What is the coolest thing you have ever done at work?
"I was there the first time a flower bloomed inside our Mars dome. A tiny purple violet. Everyone started crying happy tears. It meant that life had truly arrived on another planet for the first time in history."
Q: What does a Martian meal taste like?
"Our space-grown lettuce and tomatoes are crunchier and sweeter than Earth ones! The settlers love taco night. We grow the peppers, beans, and corn right here in the dome."
Q: What advice do you have for kids who love science?
"Keep a science notebook. Write down every question you wonder about. Those questions are seeds. One day you will grow them into something amazing."
🌍 Changing the World
Impact on the World and Community

How does her work make a difference in the world? For decades, NASA laid the groundwork: growing plants in space, studying how bodies survive in low gravity, testing habitat prototypes, and researching radiation solutions.[1][6] Those were the seeds. Dr. Saanvi grew the forest. She took every one of those building blocks and assembled them into something NASA itself called "the most significant achievement in human space exploration since the Moon landing." For the first time in history, a fully self-sustaining human ecosystem exists somewhere other than Earth. If a catastrophe ever struck our planet, humanity now has a second home.

How does her work make a difference in her community? The 3,200 settlers at Mars Base Alpha depend on Dr. Saanvi for everything. Where NASA's early astronauts brought all their food from Earth, every family here now eats food grown in Martian soil by Dr. Saanvi's crops. Where early space travelers worried daily about radiation, every child here is protected by her dome designs. Where NASA's astronauts returned to Earth before their bones weakened too much, settlers here stay for life, healthy and strong, thanks to her human health programs. "I just want people to look up through the dome at the stars and feel at home. That is why I do this."

✦   Correspondent's Final Note   ✦

This evening, as the sun set behind the Martian mountains and cast a long amber glow across the greenhouse domes, Dr. Saanvi was seen doing what she does every single day: checking on her plants. She knelt beside a small tomato seedling, touched its tiny green leaf, and smiled. Thirty years ago, a little girl in California cried because she made something alive. Tonight, on another planet, that something is still growing.
